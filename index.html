My research is organized around two primary themes

Both themes come down to one question: how can machines help us make discoveries that we can trust?

Theme 1 · Anomaly Detection

Finding unexpected patterns in high-dimensional data

4. flag what stands out

Why it matters

Most data analyses only find what they were built to look for. Anomaly detection finds what nobody expected, and can reveal the most valuable insights in the data.

Why particle physics

Particle physics is a great environment for the development and application of anomaly detection algorithms because
  1. we have an accurate but incomplete model of nature, and
  2. we have tons of data and many possible anomalies to explore.

Featured Projects

HAXAD – anomaly detection in the Higgs peak

Paper v1 Paper v2 Code Data

HAXAD is the first machine-learning strategy to directly search for unexpected new physics produced together with a Higgs boson. In simulation, it increases the sensitivity to benchmark signals by up to a factor of ten compared to a classical analysis.

I started the project and led its team of 3 postdocs, 2 PhD students and several undergraduates: I set the technical direction, built the initial code base, designed the initial inference workflow, co-wrote the paper, and ran the team day to day. Together, we built it with transformers and normalizing flows in PyTorch, plus boosted decision trees, and trained thousands of machine-learning models on the Perlmutter supercomputer. The code and data are open.

ATLAS dijet search – anomaly detection on real LHC data

Paper

Most LHC searches look for one specific signal. This ATLAS anomaly detection search looks for any new particle decaying into two jets, without choosing a signal model in advance. It combines machine-learning-enhanced simulations, normalizing flows and weakly supervised classifiers on 139 fb⁻¹ of proton-proton collisions. For certain benchmark signals, it improves the limits by almost an order of magnitude over the previous ATLAS search.

I took a leading role in the final stages of the project as one of the analysis contacts: I structured the uncertainty estimation, contributed to the validation strategy, led the writing of the paper, and steered the analysis through the collaboration's internal review to publication. I presented the results at MIT and two conferences.

SHARP – reproducing published analyses with AI agents

Paper GitHub

SHARP turns published scientific data analyses into working code via collaborative work of a researcher and an AI agent: first they design a reproduction plan, then the agent implements it while the researcher reviews and steers at set checkpoints. In its first application, we reproduced a published particle physics result to within 1% accuracy, with one researcher running two reproductions in parallel in a single working day.

I led the project from start to finish: from preparing the hackathon where the idea was created, through designing and coding most of the framework and setting the direction of the experiments, to structuring and writing most of the paper. For the evaluation, I built and ran claude-parser, a tool that systematically analyzes the collaboration between the researcher and the agent.

Signal-aware latent spaces – anomaly detection in high dimensions

Paper GitHub

The fidelity of density estimations for weakly supervised anomaly detection strongly depends on the number of features. We use supervised contrastive learning on simulated backgrounds and hypothetical signals to compress LHC collisions into a small latent space. The method improves discovery sensitivity by up to 40% over the previous state of the art, and we show that it is also effective on signal models it never saw during training.

I came up with the idea, initiated the project, and closely supervised the PhD student who implemented most of the method. Together, we designed the method from start to finish. I implemented the first version of the supervised contrastive loss, chose and simulated the data, supported the evaluation, and wrote most of the paper. The method is built in PyTorch and trained on the Perlmutter supercomputer.

FlexCAST – rerunning anomaly detection analyses on new data

Paper GitLab

Weakly supervised anomaly detection analyses learn from the data they run on, so they cannot simply be rerun on new data like a classical analysis. Based on the principles of modularity, validity and robustness, FlexCAST preserves the design of an analysis rather than one fixed implementation, so analyses can be flexibly rebuilt for new data and settings.

As the main investigator, I designed and implemented FlexCAST: the framework, built in Python on the law/Luigi workflow system, and its demonstration on a state-of-the-art anomaly detection analysis of LHC-like data. I also wrote the paper and presented the concept at the 2025 PyHEP.dev workshop.

VISPA cluster – fast analysis of terabyte-scale LHC data

Paper VISPA

During development, LHC analyses process terabytes of data over and over again. On a medium-scale computing cluster, we combined columnar processing with Dask, distributed over HTCondor, with cache-aware scheduling on local SSDs. This cut the analysis cycle of a realistic Higgs analysis with 1.4 TB and a billion events from a few hours to about ten minutes, almost 15 times faster.

I co-developed the computing framework and was responsible for the cluster's hardware (30 GPUs, over 200 CPU cores, almost 1 TB of RAM and 16 TB of SSD cache), including procurement and configuration via Ansible. Additionally, I co-wrote the paper.

Featured Tools

claude-hpc – sandbox for AI agents on HPC

GitHub

A sandboxed environment for running a Claude Code agent on computing clusters or on a laptop. The agent can only reach an approved list of services, such as its API, GitHub, the Python package index and physics data sources. It starts with one command on NERSC's Perlmutter.

I designed and developed claude-hpc with Docker and Podman containers, shell scripting and Python. It is the computing environment behind SHARP, runs out of the box on Perlmutter, and is openly available on GitHub.

EventGen – particle physics simulation framework

GitHub

A minimal Pythonic workflow that runs a full, simplified simulation chain for LHC events on the NERSC Perlmutter supercomputer: event generation with MadGraph, showering with Pythia, and fast detector simulation with Delphes. A task graph ties the steps together, so an entire dataset is produced with one command, in a parametrized and reproducible way.

I designed and built EventGen on the law/Luigi workflow system and used it to produce the simulated data for HAXAD and the signal-aware latent spaces project.

substructure – interactive jet substructure explorer

Live demo GitHub

N-subjettiness is a standard way to tell jets with one, two or three hard cores apart, for example to pick out a W boson or a top quark among ordinary quark and gluon jets. It is easy to define and hard to build intuition for. substructure shows the particles of a randomly generated jet and lets you drag the subjet axes around while the τ values and their ratios update live, for one-, two- and three-prong jets.

I came up with the idea and designed the app, built it together with Claude Code, and used it to teach jet substructure to a Stanford undergraduate I mentored.
